\section{Variationally--consistent reproducing kernel gradient smoothing meshfree formulation}\label{sec:rkgs}

The error estimates established in Section \ref{sec:error} reveal that the optimal convergence of the Galerkin meshfree formulation is attained only when the variational consistency of order $q=p$ is fulfilled, i.e. the quadrature has to be exact for the polynomial test functions of degree up to $p$.
This requirement can hardly be met by the conventional Gauss integration, since the RK shape functions are rational and their supports are misaligned with the integration cells.
The reproducing kernel gradient smoothing integration (RKGSI) \cite{ref43} is therefore adopted, which fulfills the integration constraint by construction rather than by increasing the number of quadrature points.
Meanwhile, in order to impose the essential boundary conditions in a variationally consistent manner and to circumvent the artificial parameters of the Nitsche method, the RKGSI is combined with the Hellinger--Reissner variational principle \cite{ref52}.

\subsection{Hellinger--Reissner weak form}\label{sec:hr}

In the mixed formulation the bending moment $M_{ij}$ is treated as an independent field.
The moment--curvature relation is expressed by the compliance tensor $C_{ijkl}$, which is the inverse of the elasticity tensor
\begin{equation}\label{elasticity_tensor}
	D_{ijkl}=\bar D\left[\nu\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\frac{1-\nu}{2}\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right)\right]
\end{equation}
such that $C_{ijkl}D_{klmn}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+\delta_{in}\delta_{jm}\right)$.
The boundary quantities associated with the moment field are defined as
\begin{equation}\label{moment_boundary}
	M_n(M)=M_{ij}n_i n_j,\quad M_{ns}(M)=M_{ij}n_i s_j,\quad V(M)=M_{ij,j}n_i+M_{ns,s}(M),\quad P(M)=\left[M_{ns}(M)\right]
\end{equation}
with $\left[\cdot\right]$ being the jump at the corner points defined in Eq. \eqref{jump}.
The Hellinger--Reissner functional of the Kirchhoff plate then reads
\begin{equation}\label{hr_functional}
	\begin{aligned}
		\Pi(M_{ij},w)={}&\int_\Omega \frac{1}{2} M_{ij} C_{ijkl} M_{kl} d\Omega
		-\int_{\Gamma_{w}} V(M) w d\Gamma
		+\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}} M_n(M)\theta_n(w) d\Gamma
		-P(M)w\vert_{\boldsymbol x\in c_w} \\
		&+\int_\Omega w\left(M_{ij,ij}+\bar q\right)d\Omega
		+\int_{\Gamma_{M}} \theta_n(w)\left(M_n(M)-\bar M_n\right)d\Gamma \\
		&-\int_{\Gamma_{V}} w\left(V(M)-\bar V\right)d\Gamma
		-w\left(P(M)-\bar P_c\right)\vert_{\boldsymbol x\in c_P}
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The stationarity of Eq. \eqref{hr_functional} with respect to $M_{ij}$ yields the moment--curvature relation in the weak sense, while the stationarity with respect to $w$ yields the equilibrium equation together with the natural boundary conditions.
It is noted that the essential boundary conditions $w=\bar w$ and $\theta_n(w)=\bar\theta_n$ enter Eq. \eqref{hr_functional} as the natural conditions of the mixed formulation, so that they are accommodated without introducing any stabilization parameter.

\subsection{Second order reproducing kernel gradient smoothing}\label{sec:smoothed}

The bending moment is approximated in each integration cell $\Omega_C$ by a polynomial of degree $p-2$
\begin{equation}\label{moment_approx}
	M^h_{ij}(\boldsymbol x)=\boldsymbol a_{ij}^{T}\boldsymbol q(\boldsymbol x),\qquad
	\boldsymbol q(\boldsymbol x)=\left\{1,\ x,\ y,\ \cdots,\ x^{m}y^{n},\ \cdots\right\}^{T},\quad 0\le m+n\le p-2
\end{equation}
where the constant coefficient vector $\boldsymbol a_{ij}$ is determined by the $L_2$ projection of the moment--curvature relation within the cell, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{moment_coef}
	\boldsymbol a_{ij}=-D_{ijkl}\boldsymbol G^{-1}\left(\sum_{I=1}^{n_p}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol g}_{klI}-\boldsymbol g_{klI}\right)d_I+\hat{\boldsymbol g}_{kl}\right),\qquad
	\boldsymbol G=\int_{\Omega_C}\boldsymbol q\boldsymbol q^{T}d\Omega
\end{equation}
in which the vectors $\tilde{\boldsymbol g}_{ijI}$, $\boldsymbol g_{ijI}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol g}_{ij}$ collect the boundary and cell contributions obtained by the integration by parts
\begin{equation}\label{g_tilde}
	\begin{aligned}
		\tilde{\boldsymbol g}_{ijI}={}&\int_{\partial\Omega_C}\Psi_{I,n}\boldsymbol q n_i n_j d\Gamma
		-\int_{\partial\Omega_C}\Psi_I\left(n_i\boldsymbol q_{,j}+\boldsymbol q_{,k}s_i n_j s_k\right)d\Gamma
		+\left[\Psi_I\boldsymbol q n_i s_j\right]_{\boldsymbol x\in c_C}
		+\int_{\Omega_C}\Psi_I\boldsymbol q_{,ij}d\Omega
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $c_C$ denotes the corner points of $\partial\Omega_C$, and $n_i$ and $s_i$ are the outward normal and tangent of $\partial\Omega_C$.
The vector $\boldsymbol g_{ijI}$ takes the same form as Eq. \eqref{g_tilde} except that the boundary integrals are restricted to the essential boundaries $\Gamma_{w}$ and $\Gamma_{\theta}$, while $\hat{\boldsymbol g}_{ij}$ contains the prescribed boundary values $\bar w$ and $\bar\theta_n$.
Substituting Eq. \eqref{moment_coef} into Eq. \eqref{moment_approx} gives
\begin{equation}\label{moment_discrete}
	M^h_{ij}(\boldsymbol x)=-D_{ijkl}\left(\sum_{I=1}^{n_p}\tilde\Psi_{I,kl}(\boldsymbol x)d_I-\sum_{I=1}^{n_p}\Psi_{I,kl}(\boldsymbol x)d_I\right)+\boldsymbol q^{T}\boldsymbol G^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol g}_{ij}
\end{equation}
with the second order smoothed gradients
\begin{equation}\label{smoothed_gradient}
	\tilde\Psi_{I,ij}(\boldsymbol x)=\boldsymbol q^{T}\boldsymbol G^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol g}_{ijI},\qquad
	\Psi_{I,ij}(\boldsymbol x)=\boldsymbol q^{T}\boldsymbol G^{-1}\boldsymbol g_{ijI}
\end{equation}
By the definition of $\boldsymbol G$ and $\tilde{\boldsymbol g}_{ijI}$, the smoothed gradient satisfies the following cell-wise identity
\begin{equation}\label{smoothing_identity}
	\int_{\Omega_C}^{*}\tilde\Psi_{I,ij}\boldsymbol q d\Omega=\boldsymbol q^{T}\boldsymbol G^{-1}\int_{\Omega_C}\boldsymbol q\boldsymbol q^{T}d\Omega=\tilde{\boldsymbol g}_{ijI}
\end{equation}
for any polynomial $\boldsymbol q$ of degree up to $p-2$, where $\int^*$ denotes the quadrature associated with the RKGSI.
Eq. \eqref{smoothing_identity} is the reason why the integration constraint is embedded in the smoothed gradient, since the second derivatives of the shape function are replaced by a cell-wise polynomial whose moment against $\boldsymbol q$ is reproduced exactly.

\subsection{Discrete formulation}\label{sec:discrete_hr}

Substituting Eqs. \eqref{moment_discrete} and \eqref{smoothed_gradient} into the stationary condition of Eq. \eqref{hr_functional} leads to the discrete equilibrium equations
\begin{equation}\label{hr_discrete}
	\left(\bar{\boldsymbol K}+\tilde{\boldsymbol K}+\hat{\boldsymbol K}\right)\boldsymbol d=\bar{\boldsymbol f}+\tilde{\boldsymbol f}+\hat{\boldsymbol f}
\end{equation}
in which the stiffness matrix associated with the smoothed gradients is
\begin{equation}\label{hr_K}
	\bar K_{IJ}=\int_\Omega \tilde\Psi_{I,ij}D_{ijkl}\tilde\Psi_{J,kl}d\Omega
\end{equation}
and the matrices $\tilde{\boldsymbol K}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol K}$ together with the corresponding force vectors arise from the boundary terms of Eq. \eqref{hr_functional} on $\Gamma_{w}$, $\Gamma_{\theta}$ and $c_w$.
Specifically, $\tilde{\boldsymbol K}$ and $\tilde{\boldsymbol f}$ are the consistent terms which guarantee the variational consistency of the formulation, while $\hat{\boldsymbol K}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol f}$ are the stabilized terms which recover the coercivity of the stiffness matrix.
Compared with the Nitsche formulation of Section \ref{sec:gauss}, the consistent terms are derived from the variational principle rather than added artificially, and the stabilized terms contain no empirical parameters, so that the parameter sensitivity of the Nitsche method is eliminated.

\subsection{Variational consistency}\label{sec:vc}

The variationally consistent property of the above formulation is stated as follows.

\emph{Proposition.} Provided that the integration cells are aligned with the supports of the shape functions in the sense of Eq. \eqref{smoothing_identity}, the discrete formulation \eqref{hr_discrete} fulfills the variational consistency of order $p$, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{hr_vc}
	a^{*}(\delta w^h, v)=b^{*}(\delta w^h, v),\qquad \forall \delta w^h\in W^h,\ v\in P^{p}
\end{equation}

The proof follows directly from Eq. \eqref{smoothing_identity}.
For a polynomial $v\in P^{p}$, the moment field associated with $v$ is a polynomial of degree $p-2$ and thus lies in the space spanned by $\boldsymbol q$.
Consequently the cell-wise quadrature of the smoothed gradient is exact in the sense of Eq. \eqref{smoothing_identity}, and the boundary terms of Eq. \eqref{g_tilde} reproduce the corresponding boundary integrals of $v$ exactly.
Therefore the discrete equations \eqref{hr_discrete} coincide with the exact weak form for any polynomial test function of degree up to $p$, which is precisely the variational consistency of order $q=p$ required by the error estimates in Section \ref{sec:error}.

A direct consequence of Eq. \eqref{hr_vc} is that the polynomial orthogonality condition \eqref{poly_orthogonality} holds exactly for the present formulation, so that the error estimates \eqref{h2_estimate_pq}, \eqref{l2_p2_abs}--\eqref{l2_p3_abs} and \eqref{h1_gain} with $q=p$ apply without any modification.
The theoretical convergence rates are collected in Table \ref{tab:rates}, in which the Gauss integration (GI) with $q=1$ is also included for comparison.
The variational consistency of the formulation is further verified numerically in Section \ref{sec:numerical}.
